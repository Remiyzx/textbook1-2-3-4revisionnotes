Grammar

1. Adverb Sequencing: yě, bù, hěn

Textbook Pg 103-104

In Chinese, you do not use "shì" (to be) to link a subject with an adjective. Instead, hěn (very) acts as a structural bridge.

Adverbs like bù (not) and yě (also) must be placed directly before the verb or adjective.
When combining adverbs, yě always comes first, and bù stays closer to the verb/adjective. (yě bù = also not; yě hěn = also very).

Subject (S) Adverb 1 (yě) Adverb 2 (bù / hěn) Adj / Verb (A / V)
Wǒ (I) -- hěn hǎo. (am fine)
Wǒ (I) yě hěn hǎo. (am also fine)
Tā (He) yě bù hē. (also does not drink)

2. Question Particles: ma vs ne

These particles turn statements into questions, but they are placed differently.

Particle Function & Placement Example
ma Yes/No Question: Placed at the end of a complete sentence (S+V+O or S+A).

Nǐ hǎo ma?

(How are you? / Are you good?)
ne Bounce-back Question: Placed directly after a subject/noun ("What about...?").

Wǒ hěn hǎo, nǐ ne?

(I am fine, and you?)

Grammar

1. Adjectival Predicate & The Adverb "dōu"

Textbook Pg 23

Adjectives act directly as predicates, and adverbs like dōu, bù, and hěn must be placed directly before them.
dōu (both/all) refers back to the plural subject (e.g. wǒmen, tāmen, bàba māma) preceding it. When stacked, dōu comes before hěn or bù.

Subject (Plural) Adverb 1 (dōu) Adverb 2 (bù / hěn) Adj / Verb (A / V)
Wǒmen (We) dōu hěn hǎo. (are all fine)
Tāmen (They) dōu bù máng. (are all not busy)
Bàba、māma dōu -- hē kāfēi. (both drink coffee)

Grammar

1. Identity & Demonstratives as Subjects

Words like zhè (this) and nà (that) often act directly as the Subject in an identification sentence.

zhè (THIS)

Points out something near you.

Zhè shì wǒ lǎoshī.

(This is my teacher.)

nà (THAT)

Points out something further away.

Nà shì shéi?

(Who is that?)

2. Asking "Which" vs "Where"

nǎ (WHICH)

Must be followed by a noun.

Tā shì nǎ guó rén?

(Which country is she from?)

nǎr (WHERE)

Inquires about location.

Nǐ qù nǎr?

(Where are you going?)

nà (THAT)

Notice the 4th tone!

Nà shì shéi?

(Who is that?)

3. Polite Address: nín

Textbook Pg 31

Use nín (您) instead of nǐ when speaking to elders, teachers, or someone you are meeting for the first time to show respect.

Nín shì nǎ guó rén?

4. To indicate nationality, add rén (person)

Textbook Pg 29

By adding the word rén after a country name, you create the nationality of that country.

China: Zhōngguó rén
USA: Měiguó rén
UK: Yīngguó rén
Malaysia: Mǎláixīyà rén
Germany: Déguó rén
France: Fǎguó rén
Japan: Rìběn rén
South Korea: Hánguó rén
Canada: Jiānádà rén
Russia: Éluósī rén
Arab: Ālābó rén
India: Yìndù rén

5. Understanding shì (to be)

Think of shì like "ialah" in Malay. It is used to equate two nouns (e.g. He is a teacher). Never use it to link a subject with an adjective (e.g. do not use it for "I am busy").

✅ Tā shì lǎoshī.

❌ Wǒ shì máng.

Grammar

⚠️ Disambiguation: Similar Words

dàxué = university
xuéyuàn = institute, college (often within a university)
xuésheng = student
xuéxí = study (process of actively studying)
xué = learn (focuses on result/skill)
wàiyǔ = foreign language
yǔyán = language (general term)
Hànyǔ = Chinese (language of the Han people)

1. Polite Inquiry: qǐngwèn

Use qǐngwèn (May I ask...) at the beginning of a sentence to politely initiate a question. It is often paired with respectful forms of address.

Qǐngwèn, nín guìxìng? (May I ask, what is your surname?)

2. Asking Surnames and Titles

Textbook Pg 31,43,45
Asking for Surnames & Names:
  • Formal: Qǐngwèn, nín guìxìng? ⚠️ Note: You can ONLY use guìxìng with nǐ or nín. Never say "wǒ guìxìng" or "tā guìxìng"!
  • Informal: Nǐ xìng shénme? / Nǐ jiào shénme?
Professional Titles:

In Chinese, always place the Surname FIRST, followed by the Title.

Lù xiānsheng (Mr. Lu) | Yáng lǎoshī (Teacher Yang)

3. The Modifying Particle de

Textbook Pg 101-102

To show possession, you must attach de to pronouns: My ➔ wǒ de, Your ➔ nǐ de, His/Her ➔ tā de.

⚠️ Strict Translation Warning for Locations:

When you see "of / from / at + Place" in English (e.g., "student of the Language Institute"), avoid translating it word-for-word with "zài". In Chinese, the location/school acts as a modifier hat that MUST be placed in front with de!

✅ Yǔyán Xuéyuàn de xuésheng

4. Expressing "Nice to meet you" & Hidden Subject

The full structure is: (Wǒ) rènshi nǐ, (wǒ) hěn gāoxìng. Both wǒ (I) subjects are usually hidden. You can also reverse the clauses (Hěn gāoxìng rènshi nǐ), which just shifts the emphasis.

  • Common: Rènshi nǐ hěn gāoxìng. (Nice to meet you.)
  • Reply: Rènshi nǐ, wǒ yě hěn gāoxìng. (Nice to meet you too.)
