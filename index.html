Grammar

1. The "hěn" Rule & "yě" Sequence (S + adv + A)

In Chinese, you do not use "shì" (to be) to link a subject with an adjective. Instead, the adverb hěn (very) acts as a structural bridge. When adding yě (also), it always comes before hěn.

  • I am fine. ➔ Wǒ hěn hǎo. (Not: Wǒ shì hǎo)
  • She is busy. ➔ Tā hěn máng.
  • I am also fine. ➔ Wǒ yě hěn hǎo. (Not: Wǒ hěn yě hǎo)

2. Adverbs Modifying Verbs: (S + adv + V + O)

Adverbs like bù (not) and yě (also) must be placed directly before the verb.

  • I don't drink coffee. ➔ Wǒ bù hē kāfēi.
  • He also goes to China. ➔ Tā yě qù Zhōngguó.
  • They also do not drink coke. ➔ Tāmen yě bù hē kělè.

3. Question Particles: ma vs ne

These particles are placed at the end of a sentence to turn statements into questions.

ma (Yes/No Question)

Add ma at the end of a statement to ask a yes/no question.

Nǐ hǎo ma? (How are you?)

ne (Bounce-back Question)

Use ne to bounce a question back to the listener ("What about you?").

Wǒ hěn hǎo, nǐ ne? (I am fine, and you?)

Grammar

1. Adjectival Predicate

Adjectives act directly as predicates! Adverbs like yě, dōu, bù, hěn must go directly before the adjective.

  • Wǒ bù máng. (I am not busy.)
  • Tāmen dōu hěn hǎo. (They are all very good.)

2. The Adverb "dōu" (both / all)

dōu refers back to the plural subject preceding it. It is always placed immediately before the verb or adjective predicate.

  • Tāmen dōu hěn hǎo. (They are all fine.)
  • Wǒmen dōu hē kāfēi. (We all drink coffee.)

3. Asking & Expressing Wants (yào)

To express wanting something, use the verb yào.

Q: Nǐ yào kāfēi ma? (Do you want coffee?)

A (Positive): Yào. / Wǒ yào kāfēi.

A (Negative): Bú yào. / Wǒ bú yào kāfēi.

Grammar

1. Identity & Nationality (shéi and nǎ)

shéi (WHO)

Used to ask about a person's identity.

Nà shì shéi?

(Who is that?)

nǎ (WHICH)

Used to ask for a selection. Must be followed by a noun/measure word.

Tā shì nǎ guó rén?

(Which country is she from?)

2. To indicate nationality, add rén (person)

By adding the word rén after a country name, you create the nationality of that country.

China: Zhōngguó rén
USA: Měiguó rén
UK: Yīngguó rén
Malaysia: Mǎláixīyà rén
Germany: Déguó rén
France: Fǎguó rén
Japan: Rìběn rén
South Korea: Hánguó rén
Canada: Jiānádà rén
Russia: Éluósī rén
Arab: Ālābó rén
India: Yìndù rén

3. Understanding shì (to be)

Think of shì like "ialah" in Malay. It is used to equate two nouns (e.g. He is a teacher). Never use it to link a subject with an adjective (e.g. do not use it for "I am busy").

✅ Tā shì lǎoshī.

❌ Wǒ shì máng.

Grammar

1. Polite Requests & Asking Permission

qǐng (please)

Use before a verb for polite requests.

Qǐng jìn! (Please come in!)

kěyǐ (may/can)

Used to ask for permission.

Wǒ kěyǐ jìnlai ma? (May I come in?)

2. Asking Surnames and Titles

Asking for Surnames:

Formal: Qǐngwèn, nín guìxìng?

Informal: Nǐ xìng shénme?

Professional Titles:

In Chinese, always place the Surname FIRST, followed by the Title.

Lù xiānsheng (Mr. Lu) | Yáng lǎoshī (Teacher Yang)

3. The Modifying Particle de

To show possession or link a modifier (like a place) to a noun, use de.
Unlike English, the modifier comes FIRST. (e.g. I am a student at the university ➔ I am university's student).

  • wǒ de lǎoshī (my teacher)
  • dàxué de xuésheng (student at the university)
